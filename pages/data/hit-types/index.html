How the AI Risk Score works

Every journal entry is checked against 10 signals. This is how they add up to one score.

Each signal, called a hit type, looks at one specific thing about an entry and gives it a score from 0 to 1. Those scores combine, using a fixed weight per signal, into the single Risk Score your team already sees in the general ledger table. Pick a signal from the left to see exactly how its score is calculated, with the full logic and a worked example.

How the two families combine

Risk Scoring produces one score from its 8 signals, weighted as above. Anomaly Detection produces a separate score from its 2 signals, split evenly. The final Risk Score is a simple average of those two family scores.

When a signal becomes visible

The 0.5 cutoff

A signal's score only turns into a visible tag, and becomes searchable in the Hit Type filter, once it crosses this line.

Below 0.5: the signal still contributes to the Risk Score, but no tag is shown and the entry will not appear if someone filters by that hit type. Above 0.5: a colored tag appears on the entry, and it becomes findable through the Hit Type filter.

0.5

Worth knowing: one flat number today

The 0.5 cutoff is currently shared by all 10 signals, even though they score very differently. Benford's Law Violation rarely exceeds 0.8, while Velocity Anomaly or Threshold Avoidance can easily hit 0.9 to 1.0. A natural next step is a per-signal cutoff, tuned to how each one actually scores.
