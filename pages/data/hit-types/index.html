How the AI Risk Score works

Every journal entry is checked against 10 signals — this is how they add up to one score.

Each signal — a hit type — looks at one specific thing about an entry and gives it a score from 0 to 1. Those scores combine, using a fixed weight per signal, into the single Risk Score your team already sees in the general ledger table. Click Show steps on any signal below to see exactly how its score is worked out.

The 10 signals

Hit types and their weight

Two families, scored and weighted separately, then averaged into the final Risk Score. Sliders show each signal's current weight — a preview of adjustable controls, not live yet.

Risk Scoring — behavior & pattern signals
8 signals · weights sum to 100%
Anomaly Detection — AI-driven similarity signals
2 signals · 50% / 50%
How the two families combine

Risk Scoring produces one score (its 8 signals, weighted as above), and Anomaly Detection produces a separate score (its 2 signals, 50/50). The final Risk Score is a simple average of those two family scores.

When a signal becomes visible

The 0.5 cutoff

A signal's score only turns into a visible tag — and becomes searchable in the Hit Type filter — once it crosses this line.

Below 0.5: the signal still contributes to the Risk Score, but no tag is shown and the entry won't appear if someone filters by that hit type.

Above 0.5: a colored tag appears on the entry, and it becomes findable through the Hit Type filter.

Worth knowing — one flat number today

The 0.5 cutoff is currently shared by all 10 signals, even though they score very differently — Benford's Law Violation rarely exceeds 0.8, while Velocity Anomaly or Threshold Avoidance can easily hit 0.9–1.0. A natural next step is a per-signal cutoff, tuned to how each one actually scores.
