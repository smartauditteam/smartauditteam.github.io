Internal memo · Risk Scoring

How Our Risk Scoring Works

From percentages that had to add up to 100%, to a plain Low / Medium / High rating that doesn't.

JE‑001 · the old ledger

Score × Weight

Every rule that checks a transaction — “is this amount unusually large?”, “was this posted at 2am on a Sunday?” — used to carry two numbers.

A weight, a fixed share of importance assigned to that rule, and a score, a 0–100% value worked out fresh for each transaction. The overall risk score was every rule’s weight multiplied by its score, all added up.

The one rule this depended on: every weight in the table had to add up to exactly 100%.

Original weight table · 8 rules
Rule Weight
Large Value 20%
Threshold Avoidance 15%

Dormant Account 10%

Benford’s Law 8%
Total 100%

Turning one rule off broke the other seven

Say Benford’s Law wasn’t useful for a particular client, so we switched it off. The remaining rules only added up to 92% — the math no longer worked. Someone had to sit down and hand-recalculate every other rule’s weight to bring the total back to 100%.

After removing Benford’s Law, by hand
Rule Before After rebalancing

Benford’s Law 8% — off
Total before fixing 92% — broken

The same manual rebalancing happened every time a rule was added, removed, or toggled. It was tedious, easy to get wrong, and a total that quietly drifted to 92% or 108% could sit unnoticed for a long time.

On top of that, a score like “68%” implied a precision these rules can’t really deliver — they’re pattern checks, not a scientific formula.

JE‑002 · the current ledger

Hit + Severity

We replaced the percentage table with two plain questions, asked separately by every rule.

Did it fire? A yes or no — did this rule find something on this transaction. If it fired, how bad is it? Rated Low Medium or High, never a number.

Instead of “this transaction scored 68%,” we now say: this transaction has 1 High-severity issue and 2 Medium-severity issues. Nothing to calculate, nothing to explain away.

Where the rating comes from

Two independent settings combine to produce it. A ceiling — fixed per rule, answering “no matter what, how serious can this type of issue ever be?” And how strongly it fired on this specific transaction — mild, moderate, or extreme, from the real numbers involved.

ceiling \ strength Mild Moderate Extreme
High ceiling Medium High High
Medium ceiling Low Medium High
Low ceiling Low Low Medium

Large Value — ceiling: High

Compares a transaction’s amount against that account’s own history. This rule is always taken seriously, so even its mildest case never drops below Medium.

Fires mildly → Medium

Transaction B

Fires extremely → High

Round Amount — ceiling: Medium

A single round number rarely means much on its own. Several round patterns landing on the same transaction is a different story.

$500.00

Clean, no cents — one weak pattern.

Fires mildly → Low

$5,555.00

No cents, round thousands, repeated digits — three patterns stacked.

Fires strongly → Medium

JE‑003 · why it’s easier to run

Nothing left to rebalance

The old system needed a shared pool of weights that always summed to 100%. The new one doesn’t — every rule stands on its own.

Benford’s Law ceiling: low

Flipping this switch is the entire change. No other rule is touched.

Old vs. new, side by side
Task Score × Weight Hit + Severity
Turn a rule off Recalculate every other rule’s weight Flip one switch
Add a new rule Recalculate every existing rule’s weight Give it a category and a ceiling
Make a rule stricter Adjust its weight and everyone else’s Change its ceiling alone
Risk of silent errors High — totals could drift off 100% None — there’s no shared total
What the auditor sees A percentage implying false precision “2 High, 1 Medium”

Bottom line

We removed the shared pool every rule’s weight had to fit into. Enabling, disabling, adding, or re-rating a rule now changes exactly one thing — because there’s nothing else left that needs to add up.
